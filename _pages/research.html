Secure and Trustworthy Intelligent Systems

My research develops security and assurance methods for intelligent systems spanning foundation models, sensing, robotics, and computing hardware. I study how adversarial effects enter and propagate across data, models, sensing, reasoning, system execution, and physical interaction, and use these insights to develop detection, defense, and evaluation mechanisms for trustworthy AI-enabled systems. My long-term goal is to establish end-to-end trust in intelligent systems—from models to machines.

Trustworthy Foundation Models and AI Agents

LoRAScan: detecting security risks introduced by third-party LoRA adapters through runtime analysis of abnormal activation behavior.

Foundation models are increasingly deployed as reusable components through prompts, fine-tuning, parameter-efficient adapters, retrieval, external tools, and autonomous agents. These deployment mechanisms create new attack surfaces that extend beyond conventional adversarial examples to jailbreaks, backdoors, compromised model components, and malicious internal states.

As illustrated in the figure, modern foundation-model ecosystems increasingly rely on third-party parameter-efficient components such as LoRA adapters. Although these components enable efficient model customization and reuse, they also introduce a new AI supply-chain attack surface: a compromised adapter may behave normally on benign inputs while activating hidden malicious behavior when a trigger is present. Our LoRAScan work addresses this challenge by monitoring abnormal activation patterns at LoRA insertion sites and detecting potentially backdoored adapters at runtime without modifying the underlying model or adapter.

Our research develops deployment-compatible mechanisms for detecting and mitigating these threats while preserving model utility. Recent work investigates dynamic defenses against jailbreak attacks, runtime detection of compromised model adapters, and security risks that propagate from foundation models into larger AI-enabled systems. More broadly, we seek continuous assurance mechanisms that use model behavior and internal representations to establish trust throughout the AI lifecycle.

Selected Work:

  • "Dynamic Deep Prompt Optimization for Defending Against Jailbreak Attacks on LLMs," AAAI 2026.
  • "LoRAScan: Runtime Detection of Backdoored LoRA Adapters," under review.
  • "CloudLeak: Large-Scale Deep Learning Models Stealing Through Adversarial Examples," NDSS 2020.

Secure Intelligent Sensing and Embodied AI

As AI moves from digital environments into autonomous vehicles, robots, and other cyber-physical systems, security failures can propagate from sensing and perception to reasoning, planning, control, and ultimately physical action. Our research studies these vulnerabilities across the complete perception–planning–control loop.

Our earlier work demonstrated practical physical attacks against LiDAR and 3D perception systems, including adversarial objects and roadside attacks. Building on this foundation, our NSF-supported research develops systematic security evaluation and defense mechanisms for intelligent 3D sensing through post-processing, sensor fusion, collaborative sensing, and runtime monitoring. Our recent work further extends this perspective to embodied AI by revealing how a robot's internal state and action history can become security-sensitive attack surfaces for LLM-powered robotic systems.

Selected Work:

  • "Silent Sabotage: Internal State Triggered Backdoor Attacks on LLM-Powered Robotic Systems," EAI SmartSP 2025.
  • "Robust Roadside Physical Adversarial Attack Against Deep Learning in LiDAR Perception Modules," ACM AsiaCCS 2021.
  • "3D-Adv: Black-Box Adversarial Attacks against Deep Learning Models through 3D Sensors," DAC 2021.
  • "Robust Adversarial Objects against Deep Learning Models," AAAI 2020.

This research is supported in part by the NSF ERI project Towards Robust and Secure Intelligent 3D Sensing Systems (Award No. 2347426).

Cross-Layer Hardware and Systems Security

The trustworthiness of modern AI systems depends not only on models and data, but also on the software and hardware stacks on which they execute. Our research investigates security across these layers, including information leakage, side-channel analysis, hardware vulnerabilities, and interactions between AI models and their underlying computing platforms.

We also study the reverse direction: how AI and foundation models can support hardware design, debugging, and security assurance. Our recent work explores domain-specific and reasoning language models for hardware generation and revision, while our future work seeks to combine model behavior, software execution, and hardware-level signals for cross-layer AI assurance and secure hardware/software co-design.

Selected Work:

  • "Building Reasoning LLMs for Hardware Design Generation via Function-Aligned Differentiated Revision," IEEE/ACM ICCAD 2025.
  • "Hardware Phi-1.5B: A Large Language Model Encodes Hardware Domain Specific Knowledge," IEEE/ACM ASP-DAC 2024.
  • "LLM4SecHW: Leveraging Domain-Specific Large Language Model for Hardware Debugging," IEEE AsianHOST 2023.
  • "DeepEM: Deep Neural Networks Model Recovery through EM Side-Channel Information Leakage," IEEE HOST 2020.

Secure AI for Scientific Discovery and Bioinformatics

AI is increasingly becoming part of scientific discovery pipelines, where model outputs can directly influence subsequent computational experiments, candidate selection, and scientific decisions. This creates a new security challenge: compromised models or data pipelines may preserve conventional performance metrics while introducing hidden, context-dependent risks.

Through our NSF CICI project, we investigate security and assurance for AI-enabled bioinformatics workflows. Our work studies data and model integrity, backdoor vulnerabilities in generative models, domain-aware auditing, and independent validation. Recent research demonstrates how compromised generative antimicrobial-peptide models can preserve conventional measures of utility and safety while increasing predicted immunogenicity risk for specific host contexts. These results motivate application-aware security evaluation for scientific AI systems.

This research is supported by the NSF CICI project Safeguarding AI in Bioinformatics: Enhancing Cybersecurity in Biological Data Infrastructure (Award No. 2419880).
